# Contextualiser: *Social Media and Well-Being*

- **Location key:** “PDF p.” refers to the file’s page counter; “journal p.” refers to the page number printed at the bottom of the article. In this file, PDF pp. 1–12 correspond to journal pp. 55–66.

## 1. The printing-press analogy is useful—but historically incomplete

- **Original-text location:** **PDF p. 1 / journal p. 55**, section **“The Puzzle: Does Social Media Impact Well-Being?”**, from the paragraph beginning **“Humanity had flirted with the idea…”** to the paragraph beginning **“Looking back at it now…”**. The *Jikji* correction and the comparison with algorithmic platform design below are external contextual additions; they are not claims made in the original article.

- **How the article uses the analogy:** Kross and colleagues begin with the printing press to reject a simple “technology is good” or “technology is bad” judgement. Printing made knowledge cheaper and easier to circulate, contributing to major intellectual and political changes; it also enabled propaganda, persecution and social conflict. The authors use this history to frame social media as another communication technology whose consequences depend on what people do with it.

- **The historical context:** Johannes Gutenberg developed a commercially effective European system of movable metal type in fifteenth-century Mainz. The new system reduced the cost and time needed to reproduce texts and weakened the control of institutions that had previously governed access to written information. Print became important to the Protestant Reformation because reformers could reproduce sermons, pamphlets and vernacular Bibles for large audiences. However, it would be misleading to say that the printing press *caused* the Reformation: political protection, religious dissatisfaction, literacy, trade networks and existing conflicts were also necessary conditions. The British Academy’s account of Reformation Bibles is useful here because it shows that print mattered through concrete social practices—translation, ownership and reading—not as an independent historical force. [British Academy](https://www.thebritishacademy.ac.uk/blog/how-looking-at-bibles-as-objects-transforms-our-view-of-the-reformation/)

- **An important correction to the article’s Eurocentric framing:** Gutenberg did not invent movable metal type globally. The Korean Buddhist text *Jikji*, printed in 1377, is the oldest surviving book printed with movable metal type. UNESCO explicitly places it before Gutenberg’s European achievement. Gutenberg remains central to the history of mass printing in Europe, but “the inventor of movable type” is too broad a description. [UNESCO: *Jikji*](https://www.unesco.org/en/memory-world/baegun-hwasang-chorok-buljo-jikji-simche-yojeol-volii-second-volume-anthology-great-buddhist-priests)

- **Why the analogy works:** Both technologies sharply lower the cost of distributing information, increase the number of possible speakers and weaken traditional gatekeepers. In both cases, the same capacity can distribute education or misinformation, mobilise reform or hostility, and connect communities or intensify conflict. The comparison therefore supports the article’s central claim: a communication technology has no single psychological effect independent of its users, content and social setting.

- **Where the analogy breaks down:** A printed page normally gives different readers the same fixed text. A social-media platform continuously selects and ranks content for each user, records behavioural data, permits immediate feedback and allows messages to spread through networks within minutes. Its business model can reward attention, engagement and emotional intensity. Social media are therefore not merely “digital printing presses”; they are personalised and interactive systems whose design influences what becomes visible and rewarding.

- **Deeper interpretation:** The analogy should be read as an argument against **technological determinism**—the belief that a technology automatically produces a predictable social outcome. Yet the correction also works in the other direction: saying that outcomes depend on users must not make platform design disappear. Users act inside systems that structure attention, feedback and visibility. A stronger version of the article’s framework is therefore: **social outcomes emerge from the interaction between human motives, social conditions and technological affordances.**

- **Why this matters for reading the article:** The historical opening is not decorative. It tells the reader not to ask whether “social media” as a whole are beneficial or harmful. The better question is which platform feature enables which behaviour, for which person, under which social conditions—and what alternative activity or institution that behaviour replaces.

## 2. The 2020 dispute explains why “the average effect is small” does not end the debate

- **Original-text location:** The debate is introduced on **PDF p. 2 / journal p. 56**, in the opening two paragraphs beginning **“Over the past 15 years…”** and **“To anticipate our conclusion…”**. The small-average-effect argument appears most clearly on **PDF p. 4 / journal p. 58**, under **“So, Where Does this Leave Us?”** and **Box 2, “Meta-Analyses”**, especially the sentence beginning **“One review estimated that overall social media use explains 1%…”**. The two sides of the 2020 exchange are listed as **References 4–5 on PDF p. 10 / journal p. 64**.

- **The immediate academic context:** The article appeared during an influential debate about the size and meaning of the relationship between digital-technology use and adolescent well-being. Jean Twenge and Jonathan Haidt argued that large surveys and broad measures could underestimate serious harm among particular groups, especially adolescent girls and heavy users. Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski defended their conclusion that the average association was genuinely very small and warned against making causal claims from correlational data. [Twenge and colleagues’ comment](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-020-0839-4) · [Orben and Przybylski’s reply](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-020-0840-y)

- **What Orben and Przybylski changed methodologically:** Their specification-curve approach tested many defensible analytical choices instead of reporting only one preferred model. This mattered because researchers can define both “digital use” and “well-being” in many ways, choose different control variables and obtain different-looking results. Showing the entire range of reasonable specifications reduces the risk that a striking result is produced by one selective analysis.

- **What “less than 1% of the variance” actually means:** It does **not** mean that social media reduce every user’s happiness by 1%, nor that 1% of users are harmed. It means that, in the analysed population, the measure of technology use statistically explained only a small fraction of the differences in well-being between individuals. That is a statement about predictive association at the population level, not about the experience of a specific user.

- **Why Twenge and Haidt could still object:** A small population average may combine several different effects: substantial harm for some users, benefit for others and no change for many. The average can therefore be close to zero even when a vulnerable subgroup experiences a meaningful effect. In addition, a small individual effect could still matter at population scale if billions of people are repeatedly exposed. Their objection was therefore partly about **heterogeneity and public-health significance**, not merely the numerical estimate.

- **Why Orben and Przybylski’s caution remains essential:** Subgroup claims are easy to generate after seeing the data, and the apparent vulnerability of a group does not by itself establish that technology caused its lower well-being. Family difficulties, pre-existing depression, social isolation or school stress may lead a young person both to use social media differently and to report worse well-being. Without strong longitudinal or experimental evidence, correlation can be mistaken for harm.

- **Kross and colleagues’ position:** The article occupies a productive middle ground. It accepts that the **average association is small and slightly negative**, while arguing that the average is theoretically uninformative because social media contain many different activities. Rather than choosing between “social media are harmful” and “the effect is negligible,” the authors ask what psychological processes create positive and negative outcomes.

- **Deeper interpretation:** The controversy is really about the correct **unit of analysis**. If the unit is total screen time across the whole population, the result is likely to be small. If the unit is a specific process—such as an appearance-focused teenager comparing herself with edited images late at night—the effect may be larger and more interpretable. Moving to a narrower unit improves explanation, but it also creates a danger of selective storytelling. Specific mechanisms still require preregistered tests, reliable behavioural measures and replication.

- **Why this matters for reading the article:** The article’s main contribution is not evidence that social media are harmless or harmful. It is a change of question from **“What is the average effect of social media?”** to **“Which person, using which feature, for which motive, experiences which outcome, through which mechanism?”**

## 3. Self-presentation creates an asymmetry: the same post can help the poster and harm the viewer

- **Original-text location:** **PDF pp. 5–6 / journal pp. 59–60**, section **“Self-Presentation”**, from **“In 1956, Erving Goffman…”** through the concluding sentence about consequences **“for the self but negative implications for others.”** The self-versus-others asymmetry and the limits of the active/passive distinction are restated explicitly in **Box 3 on PDF p. 8 / journal p. 62**.

- **Goffman’s relevant idea:** In *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* (1956), sociologist Erving Goffman describes social interaction through a theatrical metaphor. People perform before an audience, manage impressions and present aspects of themselves appropriate to a setting. “Front-stage” conduct is prepared for others, while “back-stage” life contains what is concealed, rehearsed or inconsistent with the public performance. [Overview of Goffman’s framework](https://web.pdx.edu/~tothm/theory/Presentation%20of%20Self.htm)

- **What social media change:** Offline self-presentation is constrained by immediate conversation and by other people who can contradict the performance. Online, a user can select photographs, edit wording, delay publication, delete unsuccessful material and display quantifiable approval through likes or comments. The performance is also persistent: a profile becomes an inspectable archive rather than a momentary interaction.

- **Why the poster may benefit:** Looking at one’s own carefully constructed profile can affirm valued identities and remind the user of relationships, achievements and positive experiences. The article cites evidence that this can raise self-esteem and help restore self-worth after social rejection. From the poster’s perspective, selective presentation is not necessarily dishonesty; it can be a form of identity construction and emotional regulation.

- **Why the viewer may suffer:** Leon Festinger’s social-comparison theory proposes that people evaluate themselves partly by comparing themselves with others, especially when no objective standard is available. Social media provide an unusually large and visible set of comparison targets. Because users usually publish selected successes rather than representative daily life, viewers may compare their unedited experience with other people’s edited outcomes. [Festinger, 1954](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/001872675400700202)

- **The key asymmetry:** The poster knows the backstage context behind the image—the failed photographs, ordinary days, arguments or uncertainty. The viewer normally sees only the front-stage result. Consequently, **one item of content can produce self-affirmation for its creator and upward comparison, envy or inadequacy for its audience.** This is stronger than saying that social media sometimes help and sometimes harm: the opposite effects can occur simultaneously within the same interaction.

- **Why “active versus passive use” is too crude:** Posting is classified as active and viewing as passive, but these labels do not identify the psychological process. Actively posting to seek reassurance may increase anxiety; passively reviewing one’s own supportive messages may improve mood. Browsing a friend’s honest account of a shared difficulty is different from browsing idealised images of wealth or appearance. The relevant variables are the content, comparison target, relationship, motive and interpretation—not the finger movement alone.

- **Who is most likely to be affected:** Upward comparison should be strongest when the domain matters to the viewer and the target seems personally comparable. Appearance-focused content may therefore matter more to someone whose self-worth depends heavily on appearance; professional achievements may matter more during career uncertainty. Depression, low self-esteem and a strong tendency to compare may increase vulnerability, although these factors can also influence what content a person chooses to view.

- **Deeper interpretation:** Self-presentation is not simply an individual choice; it produces a **relational externality**. The poster receives the benefits of identity management, while part of the psychological cost may be borne by viewers. This explains why advice directed only at individual self-control is incomplete. Interface choices—visible popularity metrics, beauty filters, recommendation systems and prompts to post achievements—can increase or reduce the asymmetry.

- **Why this matters for reading the article:** This mechanism gives the clearest reason that an overall average can conceal reality. If a post raises the poster’s well-being by one amount and lowers several viewers’ well-being by smaller amounts, measuring only “time spent” cannot reveal who gained, who lost or why.

## 4. Emotional sharing is both a support system and an outrage amplifier

- **Original-text location:** **PDF pp. 6–7 / journal pp. 60–61**, section **“Sharing Emotions”**, from **“Decades of research indicate…”** to the concluding paragraph beginning **“Taken together, these findings…”**. The contrast between supportive active use and harmful active use is also summarised in **Box 3 on PDF p. 8 / journal p. 62**.

- **Rimé’s theory of emotional sharing:** Psychologist Bernard Rimé argues that emotional experiences commonly create a desire to tell other people. Sharing serves at least two kinds of need: **socio-emotional needs**, such as validation, comfort and belonging; and **cognitive needs**, such as understanding what happened, receiving advice and constructing meaning. Emotional disclosure is therefore not merely broadcasting information—it is an attempt at social and psychological regulation. [Rimé, 2009](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1754073908097189)

- **Why social media can improve well-being:** Platforms make an audience available quickly and can connect users to people beyond their immediate location. Someone facing bereavement, illness, discrimination or a stigmatised identity may find others with similar experiences when equivalent support is unavailable offline. Responses can supply emotional support, practical advice and a sense of belonging. This helps explain why users with depression or social difficulty may sometimes gain more—not less—from particular online communities.

- **Bonding and bridging are different benefits:** Close contacts can provide **bonding social capital**—trust, intimacy and practical support. Weaker connections can provide **bridging social capital**—new information, perspectives and opportunities. A large network is therefore not automatically shallow, but the number of connections is not a sufficient measure of support. What matters is whether relationships produce useful and trustworthy responses.

- **Why the same mechanism can become harmful:** Emotional arousal also increases the motivation to share anger, humiliation and moral condemnation. Social media reduce the time between feeling and publication, remove many face-to-face cues and make secondary sharing extremely easy. An emotionally satisfying act of expression for one user can therefore expose another person to harassment or carry outrage far beyond its original context.

- **Moral outrage has two social functions:** Public anger can identify wrongdoing, coordinate collective action and give marginalised people a voice. Yet outrage can also reward certainty, simplify complex events into moral camps and treat an opponent as a symbol rather than a person. The article’s point is not that moral emotion is irrational; it is that digital transmission can magnify both its corrective and destructive capacities.

- **The role of platform incentives:** Content that evokes strong emotion often attracts rapid responses, and visible engagement can encourage further publication. Even without assuming a malicious algorithm, an attention-based system may amplify material that is easy to react to. The user’s psychological motive and the platform’s distribution logic can reinforce each other: people share because they feel strongly, while the system gives strongly felt material greater reach.

- **What determines the outcome:** Emotional sharing is more likely to help when the audience is trusted, the response is empathetic, privacy is appropriate and the user seeks support or understanding. It is more likely to harm when the audience is hostile or undefined, the disclosure is impulsive, context collapses across different social groups, or engagement rewards escalating anger. “Active use” therefore does not predict a single outcome.

- **Deeper interpretation:** Social media partly externalise emotion regulation. A user no longer manages distress alone but recruits a network—and, indirectly, a platform—to respond. This can distribute care, but it can also distribute anger. The quality of well-being produced by disclosure depends on the **feedback loop** that follows the post, not simply on whether the user expressed an emotion.

- **Why this matters for reading the article:** Emotional sharing is the strongest example of the article’s core claim. One stable human motive—the desire to share intense feelings—can generate mutual support, collective action, cyberbullying or polarisation depending on audience, norms and platform design.

## 5. The deepest research problem is that “social-media use” is often the wrong variable

- **Original-text location:** The evidence problem is developed across **PDF pp. 2–4 / journal pp. 56–58**, section **“The Search for Aggregate Effects”**: first-generation studies on pp. 56–57, the **jingle-jangle problem in Box 1 on p. 57**, and measurement validity plus experiments on p. 58. The authors’ methodological recommendations appear on **PDF pp. 8–9 / journal pp. 62–63**, section **“Moving Forward”**, especially the paragraphs beginning **“Studying psychological processes requires…”** and **“More work is also needed to validate…”**. The later 2023–2024 evidence discussed below is external context published after the original article.

- **First-generation evidence:** Early studies mainly asked people how much they used social media and compared their answers with a general well-being score. These cross-sectional correlations were inconsistent and could not determine direction: social-media behaviour might affect well-being, but loneliness or depression might also change how a person uses a platform.

- **Second-generation evidence:** Longitudinal studies and experience sampling improved the timing of measurement. In Kross and colleagues’ 2013 Facebook study, participants answered brief questions five times a day for two weeks; greater Facebook use at one measurement predicted lower momentary affect later, and use across the study predicted declining life satisfaction. This temporal ordering is stronger than a one-time survey, but the study did not randomly assign use, so unmeasured events or motives could still influence both variables. [Kross et al., 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0069841)

- **Self-reports can measure a different behaviour from the one researchers intend:** Ernala and colleagues compared ten common survey questions with server logs from 49,934 Facebook users in fifteen countries. The best correlation between reported and logged behaviour was only (r=.42); respondents tended to overestimate time and underestimate how often they visited. A self-report may capture a person’s general impression or concern about use rather than the behaviour itself. [Ernala et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1145/3313831.3376435)

- **Experiments improve causal inference but do not all test the same treatment:** Allcott and colleagues paid participants to deactivate Facebook for four weeks. Deactivation modestly improved subjective well-being and reduced later Facebook use, but it also reduced factual news knowledge. This is not evidence for a simple net verdict: removing the platform changed several outcomes at once, revealing a trade-off between well-being, information and social activity. [Allcott et al., 2020](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20190658)

- **A later review weakens the simple active/passive rule:** A 2024 meta-analysis by Godard and Holtzman examined 141 studies, about 145,000 participants and 897 effects. Within-person associations between active or passive use and well-being were mostly negligible, and much of the evidence remained cross-sectional or dependent on non-validated self-reports. This does not prove that behaviour type never matters; it suggests that the binary classification is not precise enough to function as a general law. [Godard and Holtzman, 2024](https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/29/1/zmad055/7595758)

- **The construct problem:** “Social-media use” may mean minutes online, number of visits, posting, messaging, reading news, comparing appearances, joining a support group or experiencing harassment. These activities share a platform but not a psychological mechanism. Combining them into one exposure variable resembles combining studying, arguing, shopping and exercising because they all occur outside the home.

- **The outcome has the same problem:** “Well-being” can refer to momentary positive affect, life satisfaction, loneliness, self-esteem, depressive symptoms, anxiety or a sense of meaning. A behaviour may improve one outcome while worsening another. For example, political participation could increase meaning and social connection while also increasing anger. Treating the outcomes as interchangeable can create apparently contradictory findings.

- **Displacement is usually hidden:** One hour on social media has no fixed effect because its opportunity cost differs. Replacing lonely inactivity with a supportive conversation may help; replacing sleep, exercise or face-to-face contact may harm. A study that measures only screen time but not the activity displaced cannot fully interpret the association.

- **Deeper interpretation:** Small and mixed average effects may reflect genuinely small consequences, but they may also be produced by **aggregation and measurement error**. If a variable combines opposing behaviours and is measured inaccurately, its association with well-being will be pulled towards zero. Researchers should therefore measure platform features, content, motive, audience, response and displaced activity—not just duration.

- **The present state of causal knowledge:** The US National Academies concluded in 2023 that the available evidence did not support a single population-level conclusion that social media cause changes in adolescent health. That is not the same as proving safety; it means the evidence varies by outcome, user and research design, while platform data are often inaccessible. [National Academies: report highlights](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/resource/27396/Highlights_for_Social_Media_and_Adolescent_Health.pdf)

- **Why this matters for reading the article:** The article’s call for “third-generation” research is more than a request for additional studies. It demands better-defined causes and outcomes. A causal experiment on one platform feature for one user group may offer more useful knowledge than another enormous survey asking for total daily screen time.

## Bottom line

- **Best overall interpretation:** Social media should be understood as an environment containing multiple psychological processes, not as one uniform exposure comparable to a drug dose.

- **Strongest contribution of the article:** It explains why small average associations can coexist with meaningful positive and negative experiences by identifying mechanisms such as self-presentation, social comparison and emotional sharing.

- **Main limitation:** The paper’s framework is stronger at explaining possible mechanisms than at establishing how large each causal effect is. Many cited studies are correlational, rely on self-report or examine older versions of rapidly changing platforms.

- **Most useful formula for discussion:** **person × motive × activity/content × audience/feedback × platform design × displaced activity → well-being outcome.** Removing any of these terms risks returning to an over-general claim about “social media.”
