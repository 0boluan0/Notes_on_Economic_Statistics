# Contextualiser: *Social Media and Well-Being*

## 1. Text and publication context

- **Type of text:** Review article rather than one new experiment.
- **Journal:** *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*.
- **Publication:** Online in 2020; January 2021 issue.
- **Purpose:** To synthesise roughly fifteen years of research on social media and well-being.
- **Position in the debate:**
  - Accepts that aggregate social-media use has a small negative average relationship with well-being.
  - Rejects the conclusion that social media are simply “good” or “bad”.
  - Argues that effects depend on **who uses social media, why they use it, how they use it and which outcome is measured**.
- **Platform context:**
  - Much of the literature reviewed is based on Facebook.
  - The article itself warns against treating all platforms as interchangeable.
  - US teen Facebook use fell from **71% in 2014–15 to 32% in 2024**.
  - YouTube, TikTok and Instagram are now more prominent among US teenagers.
  - Findings from the “Facebook era” may not generalise to newer platforms, features or user groups.

## 2. Authors and academic backgrounds

- **Ethan Kross — University of Michigan**
  - Professor of Psychology and Management/Organizations.
  - Director of the Emotion and Self-Control Lab.
  - Research: emotion regulation, self-control, inner speech and social media.
- **Philippe Verduyn — Maastricht University**
  - Research: social media, emotions and well-being.
  - Has studied the active/passive-use distinction and its limitations.
- **Gal Sheppes — Tel Aviv University**
  - Director of the Emotion and Self-Regulation Laboratory.
  - Research: emotion regulation, self-control and psychological flexibility.
- **John Jonides — University of Michigan**
  - Cognitive psychologist and neuroscientist.
  - Research: attention, working memory, distraction and cognitive control.
- **Oscar Ybarra — University of Michigan**
  - Social psychologist.
  - Research: social cognition, relationships and decision-making in social environments.
- **Cory K. Costello — University of Michigan team**
  - Contributor associated with Kross’s emotion and self-control research.
- **Why the team matters:**
  - Combines social psychology, emotion research, cognitive control and social-media research.
  - Helps explain the article’s focus on psychological processes rather than screen time alone.
- **Funding disclosure:**
  - The paper acknowledges a John Templeton Foundation grant to Kross.
  - It also acknowledges a gift from Facebook to Kross.
  - A disclosed financial connection does not prove bias, but readers should consider it when evaluating research about Facebook.

## 3. Historical context: the printing-press analogy

- **Johannes Gutenberg:**
  - Worked in Strasbourg and Mainz during the fifteenth century.
  - Developed a European system combining movable metal type, suitable ink and a press.
  - Made the mass reproduction of texts in Europe faster and cheaper.
- **The Protestant Reformation:**
  - Martin Luther’s ideas circulated rapidly through printed pamphlets and vernacular religious texts.
  - Printing helped the Reformation expand, but did not cause it by itself.
  - Existing religious, political and social conflicts were also necessary.
- **The Enlightenment and science:**
  - Printing allowed scholars to circulate, compare and criticise ideas more efficiently.
  - It supported scientific exchange and wider access to knowledge.
- **Important correction to the article’s Eurocentric framing:**
  - Gutenberg did **not** invent movable metal type for the whole world.
  - The Korean Buddhist book *Jikji* was printed with movable metal type in **1377**.
  - UNESCO identifies it as the oldest surviving evidence of a book printed using movable metal type.
  - More precise claim: Gutenberg’s system transformed mass communication **in Europe**.
- **Why the analogy works:**
  - Both printing and social media reduce the cost of distributing information.
  - Both weaken established information gatekeepers.
  - Both spread useful knowledge and harmful material.
  - Both can accelerate political, cultural and religious conflict.
- **Limits of the analogy:**
  - Social media distribute information almost instantly and globally.
  - Algorithms personalise what different users see.
  - Platforms collect behavioural data and optimise engagement.
  - Users are simultaneously readers, publishers, distributors and data sources.

## 4. Important academics and theories

- **Erving Goffman — self-presentation and impression management**
  - Major work: *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* (1956).
  - Uses theatre as a metaphor for social interaction.
  - People perform different roles for different audiences.
  - People try to control the impressions others form of them.
  - Social-media connection:
    - Profiles allow photographs, captions and personal information to be carefully edited.
    - Curating one’s own profile may improve self-esteem.
    - Viewing other people’s idealised profiles may produce envy.
- **Leon Festinger — social comparison theory**
  - Major paper: “A Theory of Social Comparison Processes” (1954).
  - People evaluate themselves partly by comparing themselves with others.
  - **Upward comparison:** comparing oneself with somebody who appears more successful.
  - Social-media connection:
    - Users often compare ordinary daily life with other people’s selected highlights.
    - Possible outcomes include envy, dissatisfaction, FOMO and body-image concerns.
- **Bernard Rimé — social sharing of emotion**
  - Strong emotions create a desire to tell other people about an experience.
  - Sharing can provide validation, advice, meaning and social support.
  - Social-media connection:
    - Positive route: emotional and informational support.
    - Negative route: cyberbullying, trolling, hate and moral outrage.
- **Active versus passive social-media use:**
  - **Active use:** posting, messaging or interacting with others.
  - **Passive use:** scrolling or observing without direct interaction.
  - Earlier hypothesis: active use is beneficial; passive use is harmful.
  - Article’s criticism: this distinction is too broad.
    - Actively asking for support may help.
    - Actively bullying another person harms them.
    - Passively viewing others’ profiles may cause envy.
    - Passively viewing one’s own profile may improve self-esteem.
- **Jingle-jangle problem:**
  - Different terms are used for the same concept.
  - The same term is used for different concepts.
  - Examples:
    - “Social media” may mean Facebook, Instagram, TikTok or general screen time.
    - “Well-being” may mean mood, life satisfaction, depression, anxiety or loneliness.
  - Makes studies difficult to compare or combine.

## 5. Important research discussed in the text

- **Kross et al. (2013) — Facebook and subjective well-being**
  - Method: experience sampling; participants were contacted five times per day for two weeks.
  - Finding: more Facebook use at one measurement predicted worse feelings at the next.
  - Greater use across the study predicted declining life satisfaction.
  - Strength: repeated real-life measurements improve on a one-time survey.
  - Limitation: observational temporal evidence is not the same as randomised causal evidence.
- **Ernala et al. (2020) — self-reports versus server logs**
  - Sample: **49,934 people in 15 countries**.
  - Compared ten self-report Facebook-use questions with server records.
  - Best-performing question correlated only moderately with actual use: $r = .42$.
  - Participants tended to overestimate time spent and underestimate visit frequency.
  - Importance: inaccurate exposure measures can distort conclusions about well-being.
- **Allcott et al. (2020) — Facebook deactivation experiment**
  - Participants deactivated Facebook for four weeks before the 2018 US midterm election.
  - Deactivation increased subjective well-being and some offline activity.
  - It also reduced factual news knowledge.
  - Importance: the effects involved trade-offs rather than a single good-or-bad result.
- **Meta-analytic evidence available to the article:**
  - Aggregate social-media use showed a small negative association with well-being.
  - One review estimated that overall use explained about **1% of the variance** in well-being.
  - More specific behaviours, such as harmful social comparison, sometimes had larger associations than total use.

## 6. The academic disagreement around 2020

- **Jean Twenge and Jonathan Haidt’s side:**
  - Argued that some analyses underestimated digital-media harm.
  - Emphasised potentially meaningful effects among adolescents, especially girls.
  - Argued that a small average effect may hide larger effects in vulnerable groups.
- **Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski’s side:**
  - Found negative but very small average associations between digital-technology use and adolescent well-being.
  - Defended their analytical decisions against claims that the methods artificially reduced effect sizes.
- **Kross et al.’s position:**
  - Small aggregate negative effects probably exist.
  - Average effect size alone does not explain the psychological processes involved.
  - Better questions concern specific users, behaviours, motives, platform features and outcomes.

## 7. Developments after the article

- **Verduyn, Gugushvili and Kross (2022): extended active-passive model**
  - Separates active and passive use into more precise subtypes.
  - Combines types of use with user characteristics and social context.
  - Main conclusion: active use is not always beneficial and passive use is not always harmful.
- **Godard and Holtzman (2024): meta-analysis**
  - Included **141 studies**, about **145,000 participants** and 897 effect sizes.
  - Most within-person relationships between active/passive use and well-being were negligible.
  - Active use was associated with online social support in some contexts.
  - Context and user age mattered.
  - Reinforces the warning against “active = good, passive = bad”.
- **US National Academies review:**
  - Did not find sufficient evidence for a single population-level claim that social media causes changes in adolescent health.
  - Identified both benefits and harms for particular users.
  - Recommended better platform data access, longitudinal studies, causal designs and algorithmic audits.
- **American Psychological Association guidance:**
  - Social-media use is not inherently beneficial or harmful to young people.
  - Effects depend on personal characteristics, vulnerabilities, social context, content and platform features.

## 8. Critical contextual points

- **Small average effect does not mean zero effect:**
  - A small population average may coexist with stronger positive or negative effects for subgroups.
  - A small effect may still matter when billions of people are exposed.
- **Correlation does not establish causation:**
  - Poor well-being may increase particular forms of social-media use.
  - Social-media use may affect well-being.
  - Both may be influenced by a third factor, such as loneliness or offline stress.
- **Measurement quality:**
  - Self-reported time is often inaccurate.
  - “Time spent” does not reveal content, motive, interaction quality or platform feature.
- **Generalisation problem:**
  - A result about Facebook cannot automatically be applied to TikTok or Instagram.
  - Platform design and user demographics change over time.
- **Different meanings of well-being:**
  - Happiness, life satisfaction, depression, loneliness and anxiety are related but distinct outcomes.
- **Funding and data access:**
  - Platform-funded research requires transparent disclosure and independent scrutiny.
  - Independent academics often lack the behavioural data held by technology companies.
- **Best overall interpretation:**
  - The article shifts the field from **“Is social media harmful?”** to **“Which person, using which feature, for which purpose, in which context, experiences which outcome?”**

## 9. Possible discussion questions

- How far does the printing-press analogy work when algorithms personalise information?
- Can Facebook-based research be generalised to TikTok, Instagram or future platforms?
- When does a statistically small average effect become socially important?
- Is “time spent” ever an adequate measure of social-media use?
- Which matters more: the platform, the content, the user’s motivation or the user’s vulnerability?
- Should technology companies be required to provide independent researchers with platform data?
- How should Facebook’s gift to Ethan Kross affect our evaluation of the article?
- Can interventions reduce harmful uses without removing social support and other benefits?
