## Official answers and feedback

> [!answer]- Q1 — official answer
> **Answer:** is "buggy" - it assumes that \(W \) is a set, so it only holds when \(W \) is considered a set.; is avoided by Zermelo-Frankel with Choice (ZFC).
>
> **Official feedback:**
> In Russell's Paradox, \(W \) is mathematically well-defined as a set. The question resides with "which well-defined collections are sets?" Russell's Paradox
> is not relevant when \(W \) is not a set; even Russell himself knew that it was unjustified to assume that \(W \) was a set! ZFC avoided Russell's Paradox by not allowing
> \(W \) to be a set.

> [!answer]- Q2 — official answer
> **Answer:** form the set theory that most mathematicians use today, when they want to be that precise.; are written in predicate logic.; include the equality axiom and power set axiom.; fix Russell's Paradox with the foundation axiom: a set cannot be a member of itself, directly or indirectly.; imply, by the foundation axiom, that \(W \) is not a set, since it equals the collection of all sets.
