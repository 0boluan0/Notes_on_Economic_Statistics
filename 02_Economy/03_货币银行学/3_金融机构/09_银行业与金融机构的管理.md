1. **A cash deposit.** Suppose Jane Brown opens a $100 checkable-deposit account at First National Bank with cash. The bank records a $100 increase in vault cash on the asset side and a $100 increase in checkable deposits on the liability side. The increase in reserves equals the increase in deposits.

2. **A cheque drawn on another bank.** If Jane opens the account with a $100 cheque drawn on Second National Bank, First National initially records a $100 cash item in the process of collection and a $100 checkable deposit. When the cheque clears through the Federal Reserve, First National's reserves rise by $100 and Second National's reserves and checkable deposits fall by $100. In general, an increase in a bank's deposits produces an equal increase in its reserves, while a deposit outflow produces an equal decline.

3. **Putting excess reserves to work.** If the required-reserve ratio is 10%, a $100 deposit inflow creates $10 of required reserves and $90 of excess reserves. To earn a profit, the bank can lend the $90 of excess reserves, adding $90 of loans to its assets.

There are four ways to cover the reserve shortfall:

1. **Borrow in the interbank market.** The bank borrows reserves from another bank or institution to meet the deposit outflow. Its cost is the interest on that borrowing, such as the federal funds rate.
2. **Sell securities.** Selling securities raises reserves, but the bank pays transaction costs and may have to realize a loss.
3. **Borrow from the Federal Reserve.** The bank obtains reserves through a discount loan and pays the [[Discount Rate|discount rate]]. Under normal conditions, this rate is higher than the interbank borrowing rate.
4. **Call in or sell loans.** The bank can reduce its loan portfolio and place the proceeds at the Federal Reserve, but early repayment pressure or selling loans is costly and may damage customer relationships.

Percentage change in securities market value = Percentage change in interest rate × Duration

Duration analysis applies the weighted-average durations of a financial institution's assets and liabilities. Return to First National Bank and assume that asset duration is 3 years, liability duration is 2 years, assets are $100 million, and liabilities are $90 million, so bank capital equals 10% of assets.

If interest rates rise by 5 percentage points, the market value of assets falls by approximately 15% ($-5\%\times3$), a $15 million loss. The market value of liabilities falls by approximately 10% ($-5\%\times2$), a $9 million decline. Net worth—the market value of assets minus the market value of liabilities—therefore falls by $6 million, equal to 6% of the original asset value. If interest rates instead fall by 5 percentage points, net worth rises by approximately $6 million.
