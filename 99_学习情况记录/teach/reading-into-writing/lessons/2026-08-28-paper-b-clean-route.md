## Essay question

**Why does gender inequality persist in professional workplaces, and which responses are most likely to reduce it?**

Write an essay of approximately 600 words, using information and ideas from all three extracts. You should synthesise the source material rather than describe each extract separately.

## Extract 1

Explanations for women’s unequal position at work often begin with differences in experience, working hours, or occupational choice. These factors may matter, but they do not settle a separate question: how employers evaluate people whose qualifications are held constant. One study investigated whether motherhood itself can change such evaluations. Its argument was that cultural expectations may connect motherhood with lower commitment to paid work. If evaluators assume that mothers will be less available or less competent, they may demand more evidence from them than from otherwise similar candidates. The process does not require an employer to announce an openly discriminatory intention. It can operate through ordinary-looking judgments about suitability, reliability, salary, and future potential.

The researchers first used a laboratory experiment. Participants assessed pairs of applicants for a marketing post in a start-up company. Within each pair, the candidates had the same qualifications and work histories. The researchers varied gender and parental status, so that one version of an applicant was described as a parent and another as not having children. Participants then made several decisions rather than one simple hiring choice. They rated applicants’ competence and commitment, set standards for acceptable performance and punctuality, recommended starting salaries, and judged whether candidates should be hired, promoted, or prepared for management. This design was intended to separate the effect of parental status from differences in education, experience, or actual productivity.

The results showed a marked contrast between mothers and otherwise similar women without children. Mothers were judged less competent and less committed to work. Their competence ratings were roughly 10 per cent lower, while their commitment ratings were about 15 per cent lower. They were also expected to meet stricter standards: participants required stronger management-test performance and allowed fewer occasions of lateness. The consequences extended to employment outcomes. Mothers received recommended salaries approximately $11,000 lower than those proposed for non-mothers, and they were less often recommended for management or hiring. Eighty-four per cent of women without children were recommended for hire, compared with 47 per cent of mothers. Fathers did not face parallel penalties and, on some measures, were treated more favourably than men without children.

A second part of the research examined actual recruitment. Over eighteen months, the researchers sent 1,276 carefully matched applications to 638 entry- and mid-level marketing and business vacancies in one large north-eastern US city. Each vacancy received applications from two equally qualified candidates of the same gender; one application signalled parenthood through activities such as involvement in a parent-teacher association, while the other did not. Women without children received callbacks from 6.6 per cent of employers, compared with 3.1 per cent for mothers. The difference was statistically significant. The callback rates for fathers and non-fathers did not differ significantly. Because qualifications were matched by design, the finding indicates that parental status affected employers’ initial responses to female applicants.

The two methods have different strengths. The laboratory study identifies a plausible mechanism: lower assumptions about mothers’ competence and commitment partly explained the negative recommendations. The field audit demonstrates that a disadvantage can appear in real recruitment, but it cannot reveal employers’ reasoning. Because the experiment and audit examine different settings, their agreement reduces the likelihood that the pattern is merely an artefact of one measurement method. Neither study measures every stage of a career, actual job performance, or the effects of a particular reform. It is therefore not evidence that one policy has already solved the problem. It does, however, suggest that workplaces seeking fairer outcomes need to examine how apparently neutral judgments about commitment, availability, and potential are made when family information becomes visible.

## Extract 2

Gender differences in professional earnings cannot be understood only by comparing women’s and men’s education or occupation. In many countries, women’s educational attainment and labour-market participation have moved closer to men’s, yet substantial income gaps remain in particular professional fields. One explanation focuses on the structure of work itself. Some occupations reward employees not simply for the total number of hours they work, but for working very long, continuous, or precisely timed hours. In such jobs, an employee who is available at particular moments may earn considerably more than someone who works fewer hours. The relationship between time and pay is therefore not always proportional.

This pattern is especially important where workers cannot easily replace one another. A lawyer with a particular client, for example, may be difficult to substitute at short notice. The same can apply where work depends on constant contact, rapid decisions, team coordination, or tight deadlines. When information is hard to transfer, employers may treat continuous availability as highly valuable. A worker who wants shorter, interrupted, or more predictable hours may therefore face an earnings penalty larger than the reduction in hours alone would predict. This is described as a non-linear relationship between earnings and time: reducing work time by one fifth may reduce pay by much more than one fifth.

Evidence from occupational data supports this account. An analysis of high-income occupations compared adjusted gender earnings gaps with characteristics recorded in the US Occupational Information Network. Occupations involving greater time pressure, more intensive interaction with others, and less scope for substituting workers tended to have larger gender gaps. Business, finance, and law showed particularly high returns to long hours, whereas science, technology, and some health occupations generally showed lower returns of this kind. These comparisons identify relationships across occupations; they do not prove that one feature alone causes the gap. Nevertheless, they point to the importance of work organisation as well as individual qualifications.

Research on graduates of MBA and law programmes provides a more detailed illustration. At the beginning of their careers, women and men with MBAs earned similar amounts. Over time, however, the difference grew sharply. Ten to sixteen years after graduation, women’s annual earnings were about 55 per cent of men’s. Differences in prior training explained part of the gap, while career interruptions and weekly working hours explained much of the rest. In legal careers, the relationship between annual earnings and hours also became strongly non-linear later in a career. Small differences in hours, time away from work, or periods of part-time employment could therefore lead to much larger income differences than the time differences themselves.

Pharmacy offers a contrasting example. Pharmacists often work part-time, including many women with children, but their earnings are closer to proportional to hours worked. Large chains and hospitals, standardised procedures, and shared electronic records have made it easier for one licensed pharmacist to continue another’s work. Women’s full-time, full-year earnings relative to men’s rose substantially over time, while the hours-adjusted ratio reached approximately 0.93 to 0.95. This does not mean that every professional occupation can be reorganised in the same way. It suggests, however, that reducing the cost of handovers, improving information sharing, and making workers more substitutable may lessen the financial penalty attached to flexibility. Importantly, the comparison concerns the price attached to availability, not a claim that workers with care responsibilities possess less skill or ambition. The evidence does not show that flexibility alone removes inequality, nor does it exclude discrimination. It instead indicates that pay systems which strongly reward particular hours and uninterrupted presence can preserve inequality even among highly educated professionals.

## Extract 3

Organisations often present merit-based pay as a straightforward way to make careers fairer. The principle appears simple: employees who perform better should receive better rewards. Yet a culture that repeatedly describes itself as objective or meritocratic may not automatically produce unbiased decisions. An experimental study examined whether emphasising meritocracy could affect how managers translate equivalent performance into bonuses. The researchers proposed a paradox. If managers are reassured that their organisation is fair, they may become less careful about the possibility that gender stereotypes are influencing their own judgments.

Across three experiments, 445 participants with managerial experience acted as managers in a fictional US service company. Most were MBA students or people taking similar business programmes. They were asked to assess employee profiles and allocate annual bonuses from a limited budget. The central profiles described one male and one female employee in the same job, with the same supervisor and equivalent performance evaluations. Participants were randomly assigned to read either a set of company values emphasising merit, fairness, and performance-based rewards, or a comparison version that did not make meritocracy central. They also made judgements about promotion, retention, and future success.

In the first study, involving 229 participants, the values statement had the intended effect: participants in the meritocracy condition viewed the company as more meritocratic and fair. However, this did not lead to equal bonus decisions. In the comparison condition, the female employee received an average bonus $51 higher than the equally performing male employee. In the meritocracy condition, the pattern reversed: the male employee received an average of $46 more. The interaction between employee gender and the meritocracy condition was statistically significant. Further analyses suggested that the result did not depend on whether the participant was male or female. Nor was it explained by participants assuming that the woman had received an easier performance evaluation.

The second study tested whether an unintended comparison was responsible for the result. In the first study, the lower-performing filler profile was male. Participants might have rewarded the male target employee partly because they compared him with another man who had performed poorly. The second study changed the filler employee to a woman. The central result remained. In the comparison condition, women received on average $47 more than men; in the meritocracy condition, men received $34 more. This replication indicated that the pattern was not caused by the gender of the filler profile.

A third study changed the comparison condition again. Earlier wording had given managers considerable discretion, which might have led participants to compensate for suspected bias by favouring women. The revised comparison condition removed this emphasis on discretion. Under the revised condition, there was no significant difference between men’s and women’s bonuses. In the meritocracy condition, however, men still received about $46 more than equally performing women. Effects on promotion, hiring, and termination decisions were generally smaller and often not statistically significant, so the clearest evidence concerns bonus allocation.

The study does not demonstrate that every real merit-pay system produces this outcome. It used short experimental scenarios, a particular participant group, and a manipulation of organisational language rather than a long-term workplace reform. It also did not directly test the psychological mechanisms it discusses, such as moral licensing or confidence in one’s own objectivity. Bonus decisions may be especially revealing because they appear numerical and routine while still leaving managers room to convert evaluations into money. The authors nevertheless identify useful boundaries: merit-based language may be less risky when paired with limited managerial discretion, transparent processes, and accountability for decisions. Such measures should not be treated as a slogan or a guarantee. Their purpose is to make evaluators’ reasoning visible enough to be examined rather than simply assumed to be fair.
