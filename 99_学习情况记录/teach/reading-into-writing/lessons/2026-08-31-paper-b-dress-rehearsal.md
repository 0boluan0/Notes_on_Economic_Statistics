## Essay question

**To what extent should social-media use be treated as a major cause of declining well-being?**

Write an essay of approximately 600 words, using information and ideas from all three extracts. You should synthesise the source material rather than describe each extract separately.

## Extract 1

Public concern about young people’s use of digital technology has often been expressed in broad terms. Smartphones, social-networking sites and online entertainment became increasingly common during years in which some measures of adolescent mental health also worsened. This parallel development can make a simple explanation attractive: perhaps technology use is an important cause of lower well-being. Yet a simultaneous rise in two trends does not establish either the size or the direction of an effect. It is also possible for statistically significant results to look more important than they are when researchers have many possible measures, controls and analytical choices.

Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski examined this issue using three large social datasets: the US Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System, Monitoring the Future, and the UK Millennium Cohort Study. Together, these datasets contained information from 355,358 adolescents. The study did not collect a new experiment or require young people to reduce their screen time. Instead, it re-analysed existing survey data on forms of digital technology use and measures of psychological well-being. This design gave the researchers an unusually large pool of observations, but it also meant that their evidence was correlational rather than experimental.

The authors used specification-curve analysis. Rather than selecting one preferred model and reporting that result alone, they examined a wide range of reasonable ways to define the technology variables, well-being outcomes and statistical controls. This approach was intended to reveal whether a conclusion depended heavily on a particular analytical decision. Across the resulting analyses, digital technology use was associated with slightly lower adolescent well-being. However, the association was very small. At its largest, technology use explained no more than 0.4 per cent of the variation in well-being. In other words, most differences in well-being within the datasets were linked to other factors, not to the technology measures studied. The design therefore answers a question about average associations under many specifications, not a question about a single user’s particular experience.

The size of this estimate matters as much as its direction. A negative association may justify further investigation, especially where young people report distress or where particular online experiences are harmful. It does not by itself show that regular use of social media is a dominant explanation for a general decline in well-being. Orben and Przybylski argue that, when the findings are considered alongside the many other factors related to adolescent life, the observed effects are too small to justify policy change on their own. Their conclusion is therefore not that technology is irrelevant, but that public claims should be proportionate to the measured evidence.

Several limits should be kept in view. First, the analysis cannot determine whether technology use lowers well-being, whether young people with lower well-being use technology differently, or whether another variable influences both. Second, the category of digital technology is broader than social media. It can include different devices and activities whose social purpose, content and intensity are not the same. A general result about digital use should not automatically be treated as a finding about every platform or every kind of posting, messaging or browsing. Finally, the datasets rely on the measures available in large surveys; they cannot capture every meaningful experience online. Nor can they distinguish whether an apparent decline reflects changes in exposure, reporting, school pressures, family circumstances, or wider economic and cultural conditions. The data also do not show whether effects differ sharply in daily life among users facing particular forms of online hostility or exclusion. The study is most useful as evidence about the average scale of an association in large adolescent samples, rather than as proof that no individual can be adversely affected.

## Extract 2

Facebook is often discussed as though its costs and benefits can be inferred from the number of hours that people spend on the platform. Observational comparisons are limited, however, because people who use Facebook heavily may already differ from less frequent users. They may be lonely, politically engaged, highly sociable, or simply have more time available. To separate use from these pre-existing differences, Hunt Allcott, Luca Braghieri, Sarah Eichmeyer and Matthew Gentzkow conducted a randomised field experiment in the United States before the 2018 midterm election.

The researchers recruited Facebook users through advertisements and asked them how much compensation they would require to deactivate their accounts for four weeks. Of 2,743 people who received an offer, those whose stated willingness to accept was below 102 dollars could enter the impact evaluation. The final evaluation sample contained 1,661 participants. They were randomly assigned either to a treatment group paid to deactivate Facebook or to a control group that continued as usual. The treatment group’s deactivation was checked regularly through public profile pages, and compliance exceeded 90 per cent. The design therefore provides stronger evidence about the short-term consequences of leaving Facebook than a simple comparison between ordinary users and non-users.

The intervention changed how participants spent their time. On average, deactivation freed about sixty minutes per day. Participants used less online activity, including other social media, and reported more time in offline activities such as watching television alone and socialising with family and friends. The experiment also measured a broad range of political and personal outcomes. Those who stopped using Facebook knew less about recent news: the overall news-knowledge index fell by 0.19 standard deviations. They were less politically polarised, although the study found no detectable effect on political engagement as measured by voting and clicking links to support political causes.

The personal well-being results moved in the opposite direction from the news-knowledge result. Deactivation produced a small but statistically significant improvement in the overall subjective well-being index, equal to 0.09 standard deviations. The largest individual improvements were in life satisfaction, anxiety, depression and happiness. The life-satisfaction estimate was 0.12 standard deviations, while the estimates for anxiety, depression and happiness were approximately 0.10, 0.09 and 0.08 standard deviations respectively. Several weeks after the experiment, the treatment group still reported about eleven fewer minutes of Facebook mobile-app use per day than the control group, a reduction of 22 per cent. These findings indicate that stopping use of one major platform can have measurable short-term consequences, including a modest improvement in self-reported well-being.

The result should not be interpreted as a complete calculation of whether Facebook makes users worse off. Participants required substantial payment to stop using it, and many valued the platform even after the break. The authors also note that subjective well-being and monetary valuations can point in different directions. In addition, their estimate concerns a four-week Facebook deactivation before an election, not all forms of social-media use over years. The participants were relatively young, well educated, left-leaning, heavy Facebook users, and willing to join an experiment, so they were not fully representative of either US adults or all social-media users elsewhere. Much of the outcome evidence was self-reported. Nor does the design isolate every mechanism, because the freed hour was redistributed across several online and offline activities at the same time. Large-scale withdrawal could also have different effects because social networks depend on other users. The experiment supports a qualified causal claim about short-term Facebook abstention; it cannot, by itself, establish that social media is the principal source of declining well-being across a population.

## Extract 3

Average findings can obscure substantial differences between people. If some users feel worse after using social media while others feel better, a single population estimate may appear close to zero even though particular experiences matter. Ine Beyens, J. Loes Pouwels, Irene van Driel, Loes Keijsers and Patti Valkenburg investigated this possibility by studying adolescents repeatedly in their everyday settings. Their aim was not simply to compare frequent users with infrequent users, but to ask whether an individual’s well-being differed at moments when that individual had used social media more or less.

The preregistered study recruited 63 adolescents from one secondary school in the south of the Netherlands. Participants were on average 15.12 years old, 54 per cent were girls, and all identified as Dutch. During one week, they received six semi-random prompts per day on their own phones. Each brief survey asked how happy they felt at that moment, using a seven-point scale, and how they had used their preferred social-media platforms during the previous hour. The questions distinguished active use, such as posting or sending messages, from passive use, such as viewing other people’s posts or reading messages. The platform questions were personalised: all participants reported on WhatsApp and on other frequently used platforms, including Instagram, Snapchat, YouTube or game-chat functions. The study collected 2,155 real-time assessments, with an average response rate of 83.2 per cent.

The results did not support a uniform pattern of harm. At the between-person level, adolescents who used social media more or less than their peers did not report significantly different well-being. At the within-person level, the researchers found that associations between passive social-media use and momentary well-being varied considerably. For the use-or-no-use comparison, individual estimates ranged from moderately negative, -0.24, to strongly positive, 0.68. About 44.26 per cent of adolescents did not feel noticeably better or worse after passive use; 45.90 per cent felt better; and 9.84 per cent felt worse. The results for time spent on Instagram also varied. Most adolescents showed little or no association, while smaller groups showed positive or negative associations.

Platform and activity type were relevant. On average, adolescents felt somewhat better at moments when they had used social media, whether actively or passively, although these average relationships were weak. Passive WhatsApp use was associated with better momentary well-being, whereas active use was not. For Instagram, the amount of passive viewing had different associations for different adolescents. The authors suggest that a private communication platform and a more public platform may create different experiences: reading supportive messages, social comparison, envy and enjoyment need not affect every user in the same way. Their evidence therefore cautions against treating a broad label such as passive use as one identical exposure.

The study has important boundaries. It measured associations rather than assigning adolescents to use or avoid a platform, so it cannot demonstrate that social-media use caused a later change in happiness. The sample was small, drawn from one Dutch school, and participants were relatively happy overall. Results could differ for more vulnerable young people or in other countries. Well-being was measured as current affective happiness, not as life satisfaction, clinical mental health or a long-term outcome. One week is also only a short period in adolescent development, and the researchers did not establish whether brief emotional changes accumulate. Self-reports of use in the previous hour may be imperfect. Repeated measurement improves sensitivity to within-person variation, but it does not transform those momentary associations into experimental evidence about direction. Nevertheless, intensive repeated measurement shows why claims about social media should distinguish individuals, platforms and forms of activity instead of assuming that the same effect occurs for everyone.
